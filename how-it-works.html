How it works

You don’t have to unpack your whole life.

This work starts with what’s already going right, and builds from there. Small, practical steps, not years of digging.

What a session looks like

  1. A chat about what you want. Not what’s wrong with you. What you’d like to be different, and what’s already helping.
  2. Where you’re at, 0 to 10. And what one point higher would look like in real life.
  3. One small next step. Small enough that you’ll actually do it.
  4. Guided relaxation. About twenty minutes of rest, which is the hypnosis part. You’re awake and in control the whole time. No swinging watches.

Sessions are online, for adults, and usually work over a short block rather than as a one-off.

Where it comes from

Bluzen uses Solution Focused Hypnotherapy. I trained in it at CPHT Belfast, part of The Clifton Practice, in the approach developed by David Newton.

It draws on two older traditions. Solution Focused Brief Therapy, developed by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg, which looks at what you want and what already works. And the clinical hypnosis of Milton Erickson, which treats you as the expert on your own life.

A simple picture of stress

Picture two gears. One is the part of you that thinks things through. The other is the part that raises the alarm.

Under pressure, the alarm gear spins on its own and the thinking gear slips. That’s when small things feel huge, sleep goes, and you snap at people you like. Calming down, noticing what’s working and planning one small step help the two mesh again, so they turn together.

It’s a simplification. The real brain is far more joined up. But it explains why small, calming, practical steps help you think straight again.

What the research says

Here’s what the tools on this site lean on, in plain words, with where it comes from.

Stress makes it harder to think straight

Brain research shows that even mild stress you can’t control can quickly weaken the parts of the brain that plan and weigh things up.

Arnsten, 2009, Nature Reviews Neuroscience

Hypnosis changes how attention works

A Stanford brain-imaging study found changes in attention and body-awareness networks during hypnosis, in people who are highly hypnotisable.

Jiang and colleagues, 2016, Cerebral Cortex

A long breath out helps

In a Stanford trial, five minutes a day of cyclic sighing (two breaths in, one long breath out) was linked with better mood and slower breathing. It’s the breathing in the check-in.

Balban and colleagues, 2023, Cell Reports Medicine

If-then plans help steps happen

Tying a small step to an everyday cue (“If I put the kettle on, then I’ll…”) makes people more likely to follow through, across many studies. It’s how the check-in sets your step.

Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology

To be straight with you: Solution Focused Hypnotherapy itself only has early research so far. It’s built from two approaches that each have research behind them, solution-focused therapy and clinical hypnosis.
